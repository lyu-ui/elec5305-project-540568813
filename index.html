When can pouring-sound estimates be trusted?

Most error is a smooth, container-dependent gain error in the physical conversion, not frame-level acoustic noise. That explains why the reliability score helps less than a simple time rule, and where reliability should be checked instead.

Container-disjoint score evaluation

19 recordings, 11 outer folds

Every recording from one container is withheld together. Fixed ridge 0.1 changes mean MAE from 1.457 to 1.329 cm; nested training-only parameter selection gives 1.331 cm. This post-review check uses the development/Test I domain.

Global threshold evaluation

On new containers, global ranking is worse than no selection

Combined AURC is 1.293 cm for Test I and 2.738 cm for Test II, against constant-risk areas of 1.742 and 2.479 cm (lower is better). On Test II, a single global threshold therefore ranks recordings worse than keeping frames without any selection. The score's small within-recording benefit does not carry over to ranking across new recordings.

What makes it work and fail, level by level

Following instructor feedback, four further experiments ask where the error comes from and which signal can see it. They use the same 33 recordings and 46 confirmed video labels. Definitions and four primary comparisons were fixed before the tables were computed, after a disclosed exploratory check, so this is a post-hoc explanatory study.

What this changes. The original Test I improvement is reproducible, but its mechanism is mainly fill position, and a time-only rule does better. Reliability is decided mostly at the geometry and recording levels, so shape-aware wavelength-to-length conversion and recording-level checks (gain and endpoint) are the promising next steps, not finer frame selection.

Full method, tables, limitations and commands ↗

One endpoint can shift the whole trajectory

The physical conversion subtracts the final predicted wavelength from every frame. A final pitch switch can introduce a global offset. Keeping 70% of each recording cannot remove that recording-level failure.

Detect final-pitch instability

The final pitch is compared with the preceding 0.2-second median. A development-derived 114.67-cent threshold flags clips 24 and 26. This offline diagnostic can miss failures with stable endpoints; its Test I ranking is worse than the constant-risk reference.

A terminal median has a tradeoff

A fixed 0.2-second endpoint median reduces Test II MAE from 2.482 to 1.583 cm, mainly through two severe failures. Test I MAE increases from 1.742 to 1.778 cm. The original default decoder remains unchanged.

Limitations

  • The level-by-level experiments are post-hoc explanations motivated by a disclosed exploratory check; the gain-factor analysis was added after the first of them.
  • Absolute errors depend on the assumed filling curve and container geometry. The project has no independently calibrated physical water-level traces.
  • Test I shares seven of eight score-development containers. Test II has only eight containers and differs in other conditions. The added LOCO analysis is post-review, and its folds share training data.
  • Exact sign-flip tests require a sign-symmetric null; small-sample bootstrap intervals are uncertain. Global curves are descriptive and do not validate a deployment threshold.
  • Of 72 planned video frames, 46 green water-surface bands were confirmed by the author and 26 were excluded. Blue rim/base bands were not separately confirmed. Perspective, refraction and visibility-dependent exclusions limit the visual proxy.
  • Endpoint processing and within-recording ranking use complete recordings. Listening observations come from one informal, unblinded comparison.

Sources and original contribution

This project builds on Bagad et al., The Sound of Water: Inferring Physical Properties from Pouring Liquids. Its contribution is the acoustic reliability analysis, controlled DSP experiments, failure diagnosis and reproducible evaluation.
